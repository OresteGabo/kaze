# Kaze Market Positioning

## Core Market Idea

In many real venue markets, customers do not reach hotels, conference rooms, or wedding venues directly.
They often go through informal middlemen, brokers, or commission-based connectors who:

- add friction
- slow down communication
- reduce pricing transparency
- sometimes increase the final price for the customer

Kaze can reduce that dependency by connecting customers, organizers, and venues more directly.

## Strategic Claim

Kaze helps people discover and access venues, invitations, passes, and event-linked services more directly, with less back-and-forth and fewer hidden layers.

This creates a product story around:

- direct access
- clearer pricing
- faster decisions
- better trust
- lower coordination cost

## Why This Matters

For guests and organizers:

- they can compare venues and event services without relying on a broker
- they can move from discovery to invitation to pass in one flow
- they can understand what they are getting more clearly
- they may pay less because fewer manual commissions are involved

For venues:

- they can receive more direct demand
- they can present spaces, services, pricing, and rules more clearly
- they can reduce dependence on informal sales channels
- they can keep a stronger relationship with the customer

For Kaze:

- this is a strong and practical market wedge
- it turns Kaze into a distribution and transaction layer, not just a utility app
- it supports long-term revenue through owned service businesses and optional managed services after demand is proven

## Important Product Framing

Kaze should not say:

- "We remove all middlemen."
- "We guarantee the cheapest price."

Kaze should say:

- Kaze makes venue access more direct.
- Kaze reduces unnecessary coordination layers.
- Kaze helps customers and venues connect more transparently.
- Kaze can reduce cost and confusion compared to broker-heavy flows.

This wording is stronger, safer, and more credible.

## Good App Store Angles

These are useful directions for Play Store or App Store messaging:

- Book and access venues more directly
- Invitations, passes, and venue details in one place
- Clearer event and venue coordination
- Less back-and-forth, fewer hidden layers
- Discover event spaces and services without the usual chaos

## Messaging You Can Reuse Later

### Short Version

Kaze helps people reach venues and event services more directly, with clearer pricing, smoother coordination, and less reliance on informal middlemen.

### Website Version

Kaze brings venue discovery, invitations, access passes, and event-linked services into one experience. Instead of relying on fragmented manual coordination or commission-based middlemen, customers and venues can connect more directly, move faster, and work with better transparency.

### Play Store Style Version

Find venues, invitations, passes, and event services in one place. Kaze helps guests, organizers, and venues connect more directly, with clearer details and less coordination friction.

## Should This Be In Onboarding?

Yes, but carefully.

I would not make it the first onboarding message.
The first onboarding should explain what the app does in a simple visual way.

This positioning is better used in:

- the app store description
- the website
- a later onboarding slide
- an About / Why Kaze page
- investor and partnership materials

Best onboarding version:

- "Reach venues more directly"
- "Less back-and-forth"
- "Clearer pricing and access"

That works better than a long explanation about brokers or commissions.

- Kaze Studio

## What Might Not Work

These are the main risks to watch before Kaze invests too much time or money.

### Generic event app positioning

Kaze may struggle if it is presented only as another event app or ticketing app.

Why:
- Rwanda already has event discovery, ticketing, QR check-in, and organizer tools
- customers may not download a new app just to browse events
- organizers may already use WhatsApp, mobile money, existing ticketing platforms, or manual guest lists

Better positioning:
- Kaze is the place to discover venues, reserve event spaces, coordinate access, and add the services that usually go with events.

### App-only launch

The app may struggle if Kaze expects venues and service providers to register, upload photos, write descriptions, and maintain their own listings without help.

Why:
- many venue owners are busy
- some businesses are weak at digital presentation
- poor photos and incomplete information reduce trust
- outdated availability or pricing can make the platform feel unreliable

Better approach:
- use a managed launch where the Kaze team visits venues, takes photos, fills in information, and verifies the listing before launch.

### Uncontrolled open marketplace

Free registration is useful, but unlimited unverified selling can damage trust.

Risks:
- low-quality sellers
- fake or exaggerated service offers
- poor customer support
- vendors who disappear after taking deposits
- customers blaming Kaze for bad suppliers

Better approach:
- free listing, but verified visibility
- clear vendor profiles
- portfolio photos
- response-time signals
- basic review and dispute processes
- stronger trust badges for providers Kaze has checked personally

### Weak venue data

Venue marketplaces fail quickly when information is wrong.

High-risk data:
- capacity
- price range
- availability
- location
- parking
- allowed event types
- closing hours
- sound/noise rules
- cancellation rules
- included items such as chairs, tables, security, cleaning, and sound

Better approach:
- collect stable venue facts before launch
- mark uncertain prices as "request quote" instead of pretending they are fixed
- refresh important details on a schedule

### Bypass after discovery

Customers may discover a venue or seller on Kaze, then contact them directly outside the app.

This is not a launch problem if Kaze is free, but it becomes important later.

Better approach:
- make Kaze useful after discovery through reservation tracking, service bundling, guest lists, passes, reminders, and payment records
- avoid depending on commission as the first business model
- let the platform win through convenience and trust

### Copying providers too early

The Amazon-style strategy can work, but it can scare providers if Kaze appears to copy them too soon.

Risk:
- strong service providers may avoid the platform
- vendors may hide pricing or best packages
- Kaze may lose trust before it has enough supply

Better approach:
- start as a demand engine and trusted marketplace
- use data to choose future Kaze-owned services carefully
- keep third-party competition visible even after Kaze operates its own services
- enter categories where reliability, standards, and customer trust are weak

## Market Challenges

### Trust

Customers must believe that listed venues and services are real, available, and professionally represented.

Kaze should reduce trust risk through:
- verified venue visits
- original photos
- clear contact and location details
- visible rules and inclusions
- proof that a provider has been checked by Kaze

### Operations

The hardest part may not be building the app. It may be keeping listings accurate and providers responsive.

Kaze should plan for:
- manual onboarding
- periodic listing updates
- provider follow-up
- customer support
- dispute handling
- quality checks after events

### Payments

Mobile money is important, but payment integrations can add cost and complexity.

Kaze should:
- support familiar rails such as MTN MoMo, Airtel Money, IremboPay, BK / RSwitch, and local alternatives where relevant
- show provider charges transparently
- avoid hiding payment rail costs as Kaze markup
- keep manual confirmation flows available where online payment is not ready

### WhatsApp behavior

Many customers and providers already coordinate through WhatsApp.

Kaze should not fight that behavior too early.

Kaze should support:
- shareable venue links
- WhatsApp inquiry buttons where appropriate
- QR codes for venue pages or event pages
- lightweight web access for people who do not want to install the app immediately

### Expansion too early

Rwanda, East Africa, and West Africa can all be attractive, but expansion before operational proof can create weak coverage everywhere.

Better approach:
- prove the model in one city or region first
- build repeatable venue onboarding
- document what works
- use West Africa marketing partners after the playbook is clear

## Lessons From Other Platforms

### Event planning SaaS lesson

Some event and wedding-planning tools fail because they build software before deeply matching the daily workflow of planners, venues, and suppliers.

Kaze should avoid this by:
- working with real venues before launch
- interviewing wedding planners and event organizers
- testing with decorators, photographers, transport providers, and dress sellers
- building only the workflows that make their work easier immediately

### Ticketing marketplace lesson

Ticketing platforms can become crowded and hard to monetize. Even large platforms must keep improving creator tools, consumer reach, payments, and marketplace quality.

Kaze should avoid competing only on tickets.

Kaze should compete on:
- venue discovery
- reservation flow
- event access and passes
- service bundling
- trusted local supply
- operational ease before, during, and after the event

### Venue marketplace lesson

Venue booking platforms often lose users when search results are poor, unavailable, or incomplete.

Kaze should prioritize:
- real photos
- venue capacity
- event types supported
- location and directions
- price range or quote process
- availability confidence
- direct response path

## Pre-Launch Venue Onboarding Plan

The best pre-launch strategy is to visit venues before launching publicly.

### Phase 1: Known venues first

Start with the most known and trusted venues.

Why:
- they give the platform credibility
- their details change less often
- customers already search for them
- good photos and information can make Kaze feel serious from day one

What to collect:
- venue name
- location and directions
- capacity by setup type
- event types accepted
- photos taken by Kaze
- halls, gardens, rooms, stages, entrances, parking, and key amenities
- included services
- optional services
- price range or quote rules
- contact person
- approval process for bookings
- cancellation or deposit rules if available

### Phase 2: Wedding venues

After known venues, prioritize wedding and ceremony venues.

Why:
- wedding venues have repeated demand
- weddings need many linked services
- customers usually need decor, flowers, clothing, cars, photography, sound, food, chairs, tents, and printing
- this category can reveal which service businesses Kaze should later operate directly

### Phase 3: Service providers around venues

For each venue category, onboard nearby or commonly used providers.

High-priority services:
- decor and styling
- flowers
- wedding dresses and ceremony clothing
- photography and videography
- transport and cars
- sound, DJ, MC, and lighting
- catering and food packages
- printing and branding
- tents, chairs, tables, and stage setup

### Phase 4: Soft launch with controlled supply

Before opening to everyone, test with a controlled group of venues and providers.

Track:
- number of inquiries
- response times
- which venues get interest
- which service categories are requested most
- where customers abandon the flow
- whether providers respond professionally
- whether customers still need WhatsApp or phone support

## Practical Recommendations

Kaze should start as a managed venue and event-services marketplace, not a fully self-service marketplace.

Recommended first promise:

Find event venues directly, reserve with less back-and-forth, and add the services your event already needs.

Recommended launch focus:
- Kigali and nearby high-demand areas first
- known venues first
- wedding and ceremony venues next
- service providers that naturally attach to those venues
- free participation at launch
- original Kaze-taken photos where possible
- manual support behind the scenes until the repeatable workflow is clear

Recommended business discipline:
- do not charge venues or sellers early
- do not copy provider businesses too early
- do not expand countries before the onboarding playbook works
- do not compete only with ticketing apps
- use real demand data to choose future Kaze-owned services

## Long-Term Profit Logic

At launch, Kaze should be free for customers, organizers, venue owners, and event-linked service sellers wherever possible.

Over time, Kaze can make money from:
- owned service operations in the highest-demand categories
- optional managed event packages
- preferred partner arrangements that do not add hidden customer cost
- payment-provider pass-through costs when rails such as Apple Pay, MTN MoMo, Airtel Money, IremboPay, BK / RSwitch, or local alternatives charge unavoidable fees

Venue listing fees, booking commissions, and seller commissions should not be part of the starting promise. They can be reviewed much later only if they do not weaken adoption, venue trust, or customer affordability.

## Final Position

Kaze is not just an event app.
It can become a more direct market infrastructure layer for venues, organizers, guests, and service providers.

That is a strong story for Rwanda, East Africa, and possible West Africa expansion later.
